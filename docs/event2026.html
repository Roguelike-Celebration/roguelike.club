“Unfolding” Traps: Extending Hazards Across Time and Space

Many dungeon-crawling roguelikes treat traps as single-turn events: the player triggers a hazard, experiences its effect, and moves on. This talk explores an alternative approach in which traps and hazards unfold across multiple turns and areas of the dungeon.

Drawing on examples from both tabletop and digital dungeon design, we’ll present a set of reusable patterns for creating these “unfolding” hazards.

Along the way, we’ll highlight procedural generation techniques—including graph-based, multi-pass, and context-aware approaches—that help support more varied and integrated hazard design without requiring a large catalog of handcrafted traps.

Attendees will come away with a practical framework for designing traps and hazards that create more opportunities for player choice, planning, tension, and interaction with the surrounding dungeon.

The City, The Street, The Lens: Perspectives on Building and Appreciating Generative Systems

Cities are beautiful, complex systems that have been built over hundreds, often thousands, of years. Many hands go into shaping a city's character and heartbeat, and most of the work is done completely unconsciously. Street photography is the art of walking into this temporal soup and finding moments of beauty, sadness, surprise and chance, and figuring out how to capture them to share them with others. In this talk we'll take a brief walk through street photography, and talk a little bit about why I love it (and what other, better street photographers have said about it). We'll take some of these ideas and think of cities as procedural generators - and then procedural generators as cities - and talk about how that can help us think about generative and emergent systems in our games in different ways. It'll give you some new language and structure to talk about procedural generators, perhaps give you a different angle to view your own work from, and - I hope - give you the motivation to pick up a camera and head off into your nearest city, to sample its procedural delights.

Corrupted Databases - Building an (In)finitely Repeatable Logic Puzzle in a Discord Bot

How do you make a repeatable puzzle out of a badly designed database?

That was the question I asked myself when I began designing a logic puzzle for the Discord Bot game "Telegrams from the Choir Train". In this puzzle, players are tasked with finding names and addresses of fictional, procedurally generated people, in a sea of corrupted, incorrect databases, using inferences and duplicate information as clues.

In this talk, I will walk through the design iterations of this puzzle, how and why I made it, what was successful and what wasn't, and discuss how an inherent failure of the system provided one of my favorite pieces of player agency.

it's standing right behind you: interface games as metaleptic convergences

Games that impersonate other kinds of software have a special quality -they create a geographical space in which the real world and the fictional world of the game intersect, just by referencing the fact that they know you are sitting right on the other side of the screen. Certain fictions feign that they exist in the same world that we do - this book claims to be autobiographical, this footage was found at the scene of the crime, this software was updated moments ago by someone who could still be in the room with you. These meta-referential stories playfully imply that you could be in the same immediate danger as the other characters - your own body and reality are at risk. They do not need to be about the supernatural to have a supernatural quality.

While interface games automatically create this convergence, my recent game work has been about extending the digital interface into tangible spaces, forcing players to use their endangered bodies in ways beyond what is usually asked for by the genre.

The earliest computer adventure games had mazes. Wizardry and Ultima had mazes. The classical roguelike screen just begs to be filled with a maze. Can you say what a maze actually *is*? ... Not as easy as it looks, right?

Dungeons, digging, and wells - the gamers yearn for the mines. This is a retrospective of video game holes.

“He looked into the hole, and like any hole it said, Jump.”

— Susan Sontag, The Volcano Lover: A Romance

This wide-ranging but decidedly subterranean 30 minute talk covers a variety of approaches to digital holes in games, as well as the actions of mining / digging / tunneling that cause them. It considers the undergrounds as caught up in varied histories of extraction, labor, devotion, gambling, travel and desire, as well as looks at video game digging across multiple end goals such as "finding treasure", "changing the landscape", or "progressing and moving through the dark". Some Quite Fantastic Shapes: Procedural Narrative & Terry Riley’s In C

Terry Riley’s landmark minimalist composition "In C" (1964) asks musicians to play through 53 melodic fragments in order using their own discretion around instrumentation and repetition. This unique approach balances procedural generation with authored intent in a way that can cause, in Riley’s own words, “some quite fantastic shapes to arise and disintegrate.” This talk will dive into Riley’s piece to look at how procedurally generated games with a strong sense of possibility (or “key”) utilize a balanced range of narrative tools and writing styles to make fantastic shapes of their own.

Totally Not a Roguelike Deckbuilder

I originally designed Thousand Hells as a roguelike narrative card game, but a potential publisher was concerned that it looked like all the roguelike deckbuilders. Exploring the Monte Carlo Forest: Using MCTS to balance your game and enlighten your monsters

How do you make sure that the herd of integrated systems that you've gently shepherded into a roguelike game work together into a winnable, playable, balanced, stable experience? Playtesting is expensive, LLMs are expensive and ill-suited, and do you really want to spend your limited time writing a single-use bot?

There is an option that has been used in the board game and simulation world for a long time: the Monte Carlo Tree Search. We'll explore how the surprisingly simple, practical, elegant and scalable algorithm that powers AlphaGo can run thousands of simulations to emulate the smartest character in your dungeon: the player, and show you how your game performs at scale.

We will look at using MCTS simulations to:

- prove how winnable our game is;

- test how hard our game is;

- find the kind of bugs that only appear once in ten thousand games; and

- maybe even apply a scientific approach to balance.

And maybe, we'll see how to turn your Wizard of Yendor into a chess-master: ready to respond to your adventures most underhanded strategies, instead of a mere overpowered goblin who happened upon a massive magical axe.

Baseball Is a Roguelike (no, really)

Inspiration for roguelike design can be found everywhere you look — even in tedious sports played by profoundly strange men in silly outfits.

With its deep history, discrete moment-to-moment game “beats,” and its vast expanse of statistics, baseball is a prime example of the hidden depths of fresh roguelike game design inspiration lurking in the time-worn and mundane. By kindling an openness to the serendipity of the everyday, we find that there are rich veins for roguelike design all around us.

After all, if we can figure out how to make baseball interesting by studying its roguelike nature, surely anything is possible.

Please Hold: Building a Playable Museum Exhibit About 1993 Game Support

In 1993 you could pay two dollars a minute to ring a stranger and ask how to beat a boss. Hint Line '93 put you in that stranger's chair! Players sat at a real operator's cubicle, complete with CRT, Newton's cradle and dead plant, and took calls from furious, confused, tired, and paranoid gamers about games that never existed. Your only help is The Compendium: a physical binder of over 300 pages of manuals, photocopied magazines, decoder wheels and sticky notes from the operators who had the job before you.

This talk covers how five of us built it in two months for a museum.

We invented a game company (acquired six times, most recently by a beef consortium), a console, and seven games designed backwards from one question: what would make someone pay for help? The team interviewed two operators from Sega's real Australian hint line and used their best stories, prank calls included. And we spent most of our time on The Compendium, which needed four copies with identical content but individual wear.

Hint lines were the 1993 version of the wiki and the spoiler file. Centralised, commercial, and run by people barely out of school with ring binders! Learn about Hint Line '93, the bizarre game we built for Melbourne's ACMI museum.

When designing *Duskers* I focused on creating as much variety as I could within a run, and while that made runs different, it didn’t make them feel distinct. The Art of the Long-form Roguelike Run: The Nuzlocke

Nuzlocking is a genre of challenge run developed within the Pokemon community - players catch the first Pokemon they encounter on each route, and then any Pokemon that faints is considered "dead" and no longer useable. These basic rules completely transform a standard RPG experience into an experience that mirrors a Roguelike - between the randomized tools in the player's party, and the ever-looming threat of a run ending due to losing a battle. In this talk, we analyze the Nuzlocke through the lens of Roguelikes, compare other games and genres that use similar perma-death mechanics, and look at some of the unique features of Nuzlockes that come from the mechanics and rules that would be interesting to import back into other Roguelikes broadly.

The Decades-Long Design Experiment That Escapes Permadeath and Finds a New Form Every Run

I’ve been making roguelikes for more than two decades, and I’ve come to realize that I haven’t really been making dozens of separate games: instead I’ve been running one very long design experiment. This talk follows the ideas that have survived that experiment: mechanics, systems, interfaces, world-generation techniques, and questions that disappear for years only to emerge again and sometimes find a working shape.

This isn’t a story of steady progress toward better games, rather an exploration of how curiosity, iteration, failure, and unfinished work accumulate over time. (true) Roguelikes are built around the idea that knowledge persists across runs even when characters don’t, I think game development can work the same way.

Bitwise Black Magic: How to Computer, Better Faster Stronger

There's a famous wtf moment in Quake 3 Arena's source code, involving the use of "evil floating point bit level hacking"... but what is that? Don't Save The @: What Roguelikes Teach Us About Story Structure

As someone who has worked in tv, film - and now AAA gaming, I have long been obsessed with story structures, both as a tool to use in my own work, but also as perhaps the biggest grift in the creative industries today. The Hero With A Thousand Faces, Robert McKee's Story, Dan Harmon's 'story wheel', and perhaps most of all, Blake Snyder's 'Save The Cat' all contain much wisdom, but they have also contributed to the mass homogenisation of storytelling across culture. How many times have you been reading a book, watching a movie, playing a game, and have found that you know precisely what is going to happen next, and after that, and after that...

I would like to look at how the stories we experience in roguelike games resist the predictability of story structure orthodoxy. Permadeath, random level generation, ascii graphics and turn based gameplay could all be viewed as antithetical to a gripping narrative structure, but, as anyone who has played one of these games knows, they can create some of the most immersive and emotional experiences we can have with games. I will argue that roguelikes teach us about the importance of avoiding well travelled paths (and structures) when we tell stories.
